## 1. Before mechanical clocks

- Sundials and shadow clocks: Egypt, from at least about 1500 BC. Unequal or "temporal" hours that varied with the season were normal until mechanical clocks spread.
- Water clocks or clepsydrae: Egypt and Babylon, later highly developed in Greece, China and the Islamic world. Ctesibius of Alexandria built regulated water clocks in the 3rd century BC.
- Su Song's astronomical clock tower, Kaifeng, China, completed about 1090: water-driven, with an escapement-like mechanism that released a large wheel in steps. Often cited as a precursor of the mechanical escapement.
- Al-Jazari's elaborate water and candle clocks, described in 1206.
- The Antikythera mechanism, about the 2nd century BC, was a geared astronomical calculator rather than a clock, but shows the sophistication of ancient gearing.

## 2. Mechanical clocks and the escapement

- Late 13th century: weight-driven mechanical clocks appear in Europe, regulated by the verge escapement with a foliot or balance wheel. The exact first is unknown; monastic and cathedral clocks from about the 1270s to 1300s are the earliest well-attested.
- 1364: Giovanni de Dondi completes the Astrarium, an astronomical clock of remarkable complexity.
- 15th century: mainsprings enable spring-driven clocks. The fusee, a cone-shaped pulley with a chain or gut line, evens out mainspring torque. It appears by the 15th century.
- 1656: Christiaan Huygens designs the first pendulum clock, built by Salomon Coster in The Hague in 1657. Accuracy improves from roughly a quarter hour per day to well under a minute.
- About 1670: the anchor or recoil escapement, usually credited to William Clement or Robert Hooke, allows smaller pendulum swings and the longcase clock.
- About 1715: George Graham perfects the deadbeat escapement, the standard for precision clocks for two centuries.

## 3. Portable timekeeping and the balance spring

- About 1500 to 1510: small spring-driven portable clocks are made in Germany. Peter Henlein of Nuremberg is traditionally credited, but the attribution rests on thin evidence and is disputed. The "Nuremberg egg" label likely comes from a mistranslation of "Ührlein," little clock, as "Eierlein," little egg.
- 16th and 17th centuries: watches are jewelry and status objects, accurate to perhaps an hour a day.
- 1675: Huygens publishes the spiral balance spring. Robert Hooke claimed priority, and the dispute was bitter. The balance spring improves watch accuracy to a few minutes per day and makes the minute hand worthwhile.
- 1704: Nicolas Fatio de Duillier and the Debaufre brothers patent the use of pierced jewels as bearings in England.
- About 1720s: Graham's cylinder escapement, later refined and widely used in thin watches.

## 4. The longitude problem

- Navigators needed the time at a reference meridian to compute longitude. Each hour of difference equals 15 degrees.
- 1714: the British Longitude Act offers up to £20,000 for a method accurate to half a degree.
- John Harrison builds H1 to H3, large sea clocks, from 1730 to 1757, then H4, a large watch, completed in 1759.
- 1761 to 1762: H4 performs on a voyage to Jamaica with an error well inside the prize limit. The Board of Longitude demands further trials and disclosure. Harrison receives most of the money only in 1773 after appealing to King George III and Parliament.
- Larcum Kendall's K1, a copy of H4, travels with Captain Cook on his second voyage from 1772.
- Pierre Le Roy in France develops the detent escapement and temperature-compensated balance. John Arnold and Thomas Earnshaw simplify the marine chronometer into a form that could be mass-produced by about 1800.
- Marine chronometers remain the navigation standard until radio time signals in the 20th century.

## 5. The age of Breguet and the great inventors

- About 1755 to 1759: Thomas Mudge invents the detached lever escapement. It is the ancestor of nearly every mechanical watch escapement in use today.
- 1770s: Abraham-Louis Perrelet of Le Locle is credited with an early self-winding mechanism. Breguet develops the perpétuelle self-winding watch soon after.
- Abraham-Louis Breguet, 1747 to 1823, Paris. Contributions include the Breguet overcoil hairspring, about 1795; the tourbillon, patented on 7 Messidor year IX in the French Republican calendar, which is 26 June 1801; the pare-chute shock protection, about 1790; the souscription watch; guilloché dials; Breguet hands and numerals; the sympathique clock; and the first known watch designed for the wrist, for Caroline Murat, Queen of Naples, begun in 1810.
- Ferdinand Berthoud, Jean-Antoine Lépine, and Antide Janvier are other major French figures. Lépine's bridge caliber, about 1770, replaced the full-plate layout and made thinner watches possible.
- 1840s: Adrien Philippe's keyless winding and setting system, shown in Paris in 1844 and patented in 1845. Keyless works end the need for a separate key.

## 6. Industrialization and national industries

### Switzerland
- Watchmaking spreads to Geneva in the 16th century, helped by Huguenot refugees and by Calvinist bans on jewelry that pushed goldsmiths into watchmaking.
- The Jura arc, from Geneva through the Vallée de Joux, Neuchâtel, La Chaux-de-Fonds and Le Locle to Biel and Grenchen, becomes the core. La Chaux-de-Fonds and Le Locle are a UNESCO World Heritage Site for watchmaking town planning.
- The établissage system: specialized cottage workshops each made parts, which établisseurs assembled. Flexible, skilled, but slow to mechanize.
- 1876: at the Philadelphia Centennial Exposition, Swiss visitors see American machine-made watches and realize they are behind. Swiss industry mechanizes in the following decades.
- 1930s: cartelization. ASUAG, founded in 1931, consolidates ébauche makers, and the "Statut horloger" regulates exports and production capacity until the 1960s and 1970s.

### England
- London's Clerkenwell, Liverpool, Prescot and Coventry dominate quality watchmaking in the 18th century.
- English makers resist mechanization and lose ground to Swiss and American factories in the 19th century. Large-scale English watchmaking is largely gone by the early 20th century.
- Revival: George Daniels, 1926 to 2011, and his apprentice Roger Smith; later brands like Bremont and Christopher Ward.

### France
- Besançon becomes the center of French watchmaking from 1793, when Swiss watchmakers settle there.
- Lip of Besançon is notable for early electric watches and for the 1973 workers' occupation of its factory.

### Germany
- Black Forest clockmaking from the 17th century; cuckoo clocks from the mid 18th century; Junghans of Schramberg grows into one of the world's largest clockmakers by about 1900.
- Pforzheim: watch cases, jewelry, and later movement makers.
- Glashütte, Saxony: Ferdinand Adolph Lange founds watchmaking there in 1845 with state support. Lange, Assmann, Mühle, Strasser & Rohde and the German School of Watchmaking, founded 1878, follow.
- WWII: Glashütte factories are bombed on 8 May 1945 and then dismantled by the Soviets. Under the GDR, firms are merged into VEB Glashütter Uhrenbetriebe, known as GUB.
- After reunification: Walter Lange and Günter Blümlein refound A. Lange & Söhne in 1990, with the first collection in 1994. GUB becomes Glashütte Original, owned by Swatch Group since 2000. Nomos, founded 1990, and others follow.

### United States
- 1850: Aaron Lufkin Dennison and partners found what becomes the Waltham company, moving to Waltham, Massachusetts in 1854. They pioneer the "American system" of machine-made interchangeable parts.
- Elgin, 1864; Hamilton, 1892, Lancaster, Pennsylvania; Illinois, Howard, Hampden and others produce millions of pocket watches.
- 1891: a collision at Kipton, Ohio, blamed on an engineer's watch, leads railroads to adopt time service standards organized by Webb C. Ball. Typical requirements included size 16 or 18, at least 17 jewels and later 19 or more, adjustment to temperature, isochronism and five positions, lever setting, open face with the crown at 12, and accuracy within about 30 seconds per week.
- Ingersoll's "Dollar Watch," from 1896, democratizes ownership.
- Bulova, founded 1875 in New York, becomes a major brand and an early radio advertiser.
- Timex descends from the Waterbury Clock Company of 1854; postwar Timex sells cheap, durable pin-lever watches in enormous numbers.
- American watch manufacturing collapses after WWII under Swiss and later Japanese competition. Hamilton is sold to the Swiss SSIH in the early 1970s, and Elgin and Waltham as manufacturers are gone by the 1960s.

### Japan
- Kintaro Hattori founds K. Hattori, later Seiko, in Tokyo in 1881. Seikosha factory in 1892. First Japanese wristwatch, the Laurel, 1913.
- Citizen descends from the Shokosha Watch Research Institute, 1918; the Citizen name dates from 1924.
- Seiko's two production arms, Suwa Seikosha and Daini Seikosha, compete internally. Grand Seiko, 1960, starts at Suwa; King Seiko, 1961, at Daini. The rivalry drives rapid gains in precision through the 1960s.
- Seiko competes in Swiss observatory chronometer trials in the late 1960s with strong results, after which the trials were discontinued. Exact rankings are disputed, so check sources before quoting them.
- Casio, founded 1946, enters watches in 1974 with the Casiotron.
- Orient, founded in 1950 from earlier roots, is now part of Seiko Epson.

### Soviet Union and Russia
- 1930: the USSR buys the machinery of the bankrupt Dueber-Hampden company of Canton, Ohio, and founds the First State Watch Factory in Moscow, later known for the Poljot brand.
- Other major factories: Second Moscow Watch Factory, or Slava; Petrodvorets, or Raketa, in Saint Petersburg; Chistopol, or Vostok, known for the Komandirskie and Amphibia; Chelyabinsk, or Molnija.
- 1961: Yuri Gagarin's flight; he is generally said to have worn a Sturmanskie. 1965: Alexei Leonov wears a Strela chronograph on the first spacewalk.
- Poljot 3133 chronograph, from 1979, derives from the Valjoux 7734.

### China
- Tianjin Watch Factory, later Sea-Gull, produces the Wuyi in 1955. In 1961 China acquires the tooling for the Venus 175 chronograph, which becomes the basis of the Sea-Gull ST19, still produced.
- Shanghai, Beijing and other state factories follow. China today is the world's largest producer of watches by volume and of many components.

### Other notable centers
- Italy: Panerai of Florence, a supplier of instruments and watches to the Italian navy. Its Radiomir lume was patented in 1916, and the Luminor followed in 1949.
- Austria and Bohemia: Viennese regulators and Biedermeier clocks.
- The Netherlands: early pendulum clocks after Huygens, and Friesland clocks.

## 7. The wristwatch takes over

- 1868: Patek Philippe makes a wristwatch for Countess Koscowicz of Hungary. It is often called the first Swiss wristwatch; Patek's records show it was sold to her in 1876. It is jewelry, with key winding and setting.
- Late 19th century: wristlets, pocket watches strapped to the wrist in leather cups, appear among soldiers, notably in the Boer War.
- 1904: Louis Cartier designs a wristwatch for aviator Alberto Santos-Dumont. Cartier sells the Santos commercially from 1911.
- 1905: Hans Wilsdorf and Alfred Davis found Wilsdorf & Davis in London. The Rolex name is registered in 1908, and the company moves to Geneva around 1919 to 1920.
- 1910: a Rolex is the first wristwatch to receive a Swiss official chronometer certificate. In 1914 one earns a Kew Class A certificate.
- WWI: officers' trench watches with wire lugs and luminous dials make wristwatches acceptable for men. By the 1920s wristwatches outsell pocket watches in many markets.
- 1917: Cartier Tank designed; sold from 1919.
- 1923: Patek Philippe's first split-seconds chronograph wristwatch. John Harwood patents a self-winding wristwatch, produced with Fortis from about 1928.
- 1925: Patek Philippe's first perpetual calendar wristwatch, made from a movement originally intended for a pendant watch.
- 1926: the Rolex Oyster case, with screwed bezel, caseback and crown. 1927: Mercedes Gleitze wears one on a widely advertised Channel swim attempt.
- 1931: the Rolex Perpetual, a free 360 degree central rotor, the template for nearly every automatic since. Jaeger-LeCoultre Reverso for polo players.
- 1932: Patek Philippe Calatrava ref. 96, the archetypal round dress watch, the year the Stern family acquires Patek.
- 1930s: Louis Cottier's world time mechanism.
- 1934: Incabloc shock protection.

## 8. Tool watches and the mid-century golden age

- 1945: Rolex Datejust. 1948: Omega Seamaster.
- 1953: Blancpain Fifty Fathoms, developed with French navy combat divers Robert Maloubier and Claude Riffaud. Rolex Submariner introduced around 1953 to 1954. Rolex Explorer associated with the 1953 Everest expedition. Rolex Cyclops date lens.
- 1954 to 1955: Rolex GMT-Master developed with Pan Am. Jaeger-LeCoultre Memovox automatic alarm, 1956.
- 1956: Rolex Day-Date. Rolex Milgauss for scientists working near magnetic fields.
- 1957: Omega's trilogy of Speedmaster CK2915, Seamaster 300 and Railmaster. Hamilton Electric 500, the first battery-powered wristwatch.
- 1960: Bulova Accutron tuning-fork watch. First Grand Seiko.
- 1962: Wally Schirra wears his personal Omega CK2998 on Mercury-Atlas 8.
- 1963: Rolex Cosmograph, later Daytona. Heuer Carrera, designed by Jack Heuer. Seiko 5 line begins.
- 1965: NASA flight-qualifies the Omega Speedmaster after testing several brands. Seiko 62MAS, its first diver.
- 1967: Rolex Sea-Dweller development begins with COMEX; helium escape valves follow. Doxa SUB 300, 1967.
- 1968: Seiko and the high-beat era: 36,000 vph Grand Seiko and King Seiko models.
- 20 July 1969: Buzz Aldrin wears Omega Speedmaster ST105.012 on the lunar surface. Neil Armstrong left his inside the lunar module as a backup timer. Aldrin's watch later went missing.
- 1969, the automatic chronograph race, see section 13.

## 9. Electric, electronic and quartz

- 1952: Lip and Elgin show electric watch prototypes.
- 1957: Hamilton Electric 500, a balance wheel driven by a battery-powered coil.
- 1960: Bulova Accutron, designed by Max Hetzel, using a 360 Hz tuning fork. Accurate to about a minute a month and famous for its hum.
- 1967: the Centre Electronique Horloger in Neuchâtel shows the Beta 1 and Beta 2 quartz prototypes. Seiko shows quartz prototypes the same year.
- 25 December 1969: Seiko Astron 35SQ goes on sale in Tokyo, the first quartz wristwatch sold.
- 1970: the Swiss Beta 21 quartz movement appears in watches from about 16 brands, including Omega Electroquartz. Hamilton announces the Pulsar LED digital watch in May 1970; it goes on sale in 1972.
- 1972 to 1973: LCD digital watches appear, and prices collapse within a decade.
- 1970s: Seiko and Citizen industrialize the quartz analog watch with stepper motors; Japanese and Hong Kong makers dominate volume.

## 10. The quartz crisis

- Causes: cheap and accurate quartz, the end of the Bretton Woods fixed exchange rates which let the Swiss franc rise sharply, the 1970s recessions, and Swiss reluctance to move volume production to electronics.
- Scale: Swiss watch employment falls from roughly 90,000 in 1970 to around 30,000 by the mid 1980s; sources range from 28,000 to 33,000 depending on year and definition. The number of Swiss watch firms falls from about 1,600 to about 600.
- Many famous names disappear, shrink, or drift into dormancy over the following decades, including Universal Genève, Gallet, and dozens of movement makers.
- 1983: banks bring in consultant Nicolas Hayek. ASUAG and SSIH merge into SMH, renamed The Swatch Group in 1998. The plastic Swatch launches in 1983, designed by Elmar Mock and Jacques Müller with 51 parts and an ultrasonically welded case.
- The same year, Casio's Kikuo Ibe launches the G-Shock DW-5000C.

## 11. The mechanical renaissance

- 1972: Audemars Piguet Royal Oak ref. 5402, designed by Gérald Genta, a steel luxury sports watch priced like gold. 1976: Patek Philippe Nautilus 3700 and IWC Ingenieur SL, also Genta.
- 1980s: Jean-Claude Biver and Jacques Piguet revive Blancpain as a purely mechanical brand from 1982, with marketing that claimed it had never made a quartz watch.
- 1989: Patek Philippe Calibre 89 for the firm's 150th anniversary, then the most complicated portable watch.
- 1990s: mechanical watches are recast as luxury and heritage objects. A. Lange & Söhne returns in 1994. Independents like F.P. Journe, founded 1999, and Philippe Dufour gain cult followings. The Fondation de la Haute Horlogerie is founded in 2005.
- 1996: Patek Philippe 5035, the first annual calendar.
- 1999: Omega launches the co-axial escapement, invented by George Daniels in the 1970s. Seiko launches Spring Drive commercially.
- 2001: Ulysse Nardin Freak introduces silicon escapement parts; silicon spreads through the industry in the 2000s.
- Mid 2000s onward: in-house movement programs proliferate, partly in response to ETA reducing supply to non-Swatch brands from the early 2000s.
- 2010s: a boom in steel sports watches, waitlists, and grey market premiums, peaking in early 2022 before a broad price correction.

## 12. The 21st century

- 2015: Apple Watch on sale. By 2019 analysts estimated it outsold the entire Swiss industry by unit volume. Omega and METAS launch the Master Chronometer standard. Rolex redefines Superlative Chronometer as minus 2 to plus 2 seconds per day after casing. Rolex caliber 3235 debuts.
- 2017: Swiss Made rules tighten. Grand Seiko becomes an independent brand.
- 2019: Patek Philippe 6300A-010 Grandmaster Chime sells for CHF 31 million at the Only Watch charity auction, the highest price for any wristwatch at auction as of the snapshot.
- 2022: Omega x Swatch MoonSwatch causes queues worldwide. Rolex announces its Certified Pre-Owned program, first through Bucherer.
- 2022: the Glashütte origin rule becomes law.
- 2023: Rolex announces it will acquire the Bucherer retail group. Breitling buys Universal Genève.
- 2025: Breitling buys Gallet and forms a "House of Brands" umbrella. Rolex launches the Land-Dweller with caliber 7135, a 36,000 vph movement with a new Dynapulse escapement. Phillips sells a 1943 steel Patek Philippe 1518 for CHF 14.19 million in November, a record for a vintage Patek wristwatch.
- 2025 to 2026: US tariffs on Swiss imports swing repeatedly, from 39 percent in August 2025 to lower rates later; treat any current figure as volatile.
- 2026: Rolex marks 100 years of the Oyster; Patek marks 50 years of the Nautilus. Richemont completes the sale of Baume & Mercier to the Damiani Group in July. Universal Genève relaunches under Breitling.

## 13. Disputed "firsts" at a glance

| Claim | Competing answers |
|---|---|
| First watch | Henlein of Nuremberg about 1505 to 1510 is traditional; other German and Italian makers made small spring-driven clocks around the same time. |
| First wristwatch | Breguet for Caroline Murat, begun 1810; Patek Philippe for Countess Koscowicz, 1868; earlier bracelet watches for Elizabeth I are recorded but do not survive. |
| First men's wristwatch | Military wristlets in the 1880s and 1890s; Cartier Santos 1904. |
| First waterproof watch | Rolex Oyster 1926 was the first commercially successful one; Hans Wilsdorf bought earlier patents, and François Borgel and others made sealed cases before. |
| First automatic wristwatch | Harwood patent 1923, sold from about 1928; Rolex Perpetual 1931 created the modern rotor. |
| First dive watch | Rolex Oyster and Panerai prototypes of the 1930s; Omega Marine 1932; Blancpain Fifty Fathoms 1953 and Rolex Submariner 1953 to 1954 as the first modern dive watches. Partisans of each brand argue this hard. |
| First automatic chronograph, 1969 | Zenith El Primero, announced 10 January 1969, integrated, 36,000 vph; the Calibre 11 consortium of Heuer, Breitling, Hamilton-Buren and Dubois-Dépraz, announced March 1969, modular micro-rotor; Seiko 6139, on sale in Japan around May 1969, integrated with vertical clutch. Which was "first" depends on whether you count announcement, delivery or sale. |
| First watch on the Moon | Omega Speedmaster on Aldrin's wrist, 1969. Armstrong's stayed in the lunar module. |
| First quartz wristwatch | Seiko Astron, sold 25 December 1969. Swiss CEH prototypes won observatory trials in 1967. |
| First smartwatch | Depends on definition: Seiko and Casio data-bank and computer watches of the 1980s, Seiko Ruputer 1998, IBM Linux watch prototype 2000, Pebble 2013, Apple Watch 2015. |
