## 1. Kinds of price

Always label which one you are citing.

- Retail or list price: what the brand charges new. Changes at least yearly, often more.
- Grey market price: new or unworn watches sold outside authorized channels. Can be above or below retail.
- Asking price: what sellers list for, as on Chrono24. Often higher than what watches actually sell for.
- Transaction price: what actually sold, as in auction results, eBay sold listings and dealer sale data.
- Hammer price versus total price: at auction, the hammer price excludes the buyer's premium. Published results usually include the premium.
- Insurance or replacement value: usually retail or above; not a resale value.
- Trade-in or buy price: what a dealer will pay, typically well below asking.

Useful data sources: WatchCharts indices and model histories, Chrono24's price history tools, auction house archives, eBay sold listings, and dealer sold archives. See `research-playbook.md`.

## 2. Where to buy and the trade-offs

| Channel | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| Authorized dealer or boutique | Full warranty, guaranteed authenticity, possible discount on less popular models | Waitlists and allocation for hot models, less negotiation on popular brands |
| Grey market dealer | Immediate availability, discounts on many brands | Warranty may be dealer-only or start early, dealer reputation is everything |
| Certified pre-owned from the brand | Brand-checked authenticity and warranty | Premium over other pre-owned |
| Established pre-owned dealer | Selection, return policies, in-house watchmakers | Margins built into price |
| Marketplaces such as Chrono24 and eBay | Huge selection, buyer protection programs | Quality of sellers varies, fakes and scams exist |
| Forum and private sales | Good prices, knowledgeable sellers | Little protection; rely on reputation, references and payment safeguards |
| Auction | Rare pieces, published results, specialist catalog descriptions | Buyer's premium, auction fever, condition reports may be optimistic, limited recourse |

## 3. Transaction costs

- Auction buyer's premium: commonly 25 percent or more of hammer at the top houses, often tiered, plus taxes. Check each house's current schedule.
- Seller's commission at auction: negotiable for valuable consignments.
- Marketplace fees: seller fees and buyer protection fees on platforms.
- Dealer spread: the difference between what a dealer pays and charges is often 15 to 30 percent or more.
- Import duty and VAT or sales tax: depend on country, origin, and value. Tariffs are volatile.
- Shipping and insurance: fully insured, signature-required shipping.
- A watch typically needs to appreciate meaningfully just to break even after round-trip costs.

## 4. What drives value

Roughly in order for vintage pieces:

1. Originality: original dial, hands, bezel insert, crown, crystal type and bracelet for the period. A redial or service dial can cut value by half or more on collectible references.
2. Dial condition: the most important visual element. Even patina is good; spotting, water damage and flaking are bad.
3. Case condition: unpolished or lightly polished cases with sharp edges and original brushing command large premiums.
4. Rarity: a scarce variant, such as a short-lived dial or a military issue, matters only when collectors recognize and want it.
5. Provenance: famous owners, original receipts, military records, archive extracts.
6. Box and papers: matter far more for modern watches; for vintage, papers add value but originality comes first.
7. Service history: documented service by the brand or a respected independent, ideally with original parts returned.
8. Desirability: the reference's reputation and current fashion.

For modern watches: completeness of the set, condition, warranty status, and current demand dominate.

## 5. Condition vocabulary

There is no universal grading scale. Terms used by dealers and auction houses:

- New or unworn: never worn, often with stickers.
- NOS, new old stock: vintage, never sold, never worn.
- Mint or near mint: no visible wear.
- Excellent: minor wear visible under close inspection.
- Very good: normal light wear.
- Good or fair: obvious wear, marks or issues.
- Project or for parts: not running or seriously damaged.

Always ask for photos under good light, the movement, and recent timegrapher results. Descriptions like "serviced" mean little without a date, a receipt and the watchmaker's name.

## 6. Papers, boxes and provenance

- Papers: the warranty card or certificate, dated and stamped by the dealer. Rolex moved from paper certificates to plastic warranty cards in the 2000s and digital warranty records later.
- Box and accessories: inner and outer boxes, hang tags, booklets, bracelet links. Missing links reduce value and wearability.
- Archive extracts: Patek Philippe, Omega, Longines, IWC, Vacheron Constantin, Jaeger-LeCoultre, Audemars Piguet, Zenith and others issue extracts confirming production date, original configuration and sometimes the original destination. See `identification-and-dating.md`.
- Fakes extend to papers and boxes. Papers alone do not prove authenticity, and "box and papers" sets are sometimes married to a different watch. Check that serial numbers match.

## 7. Market cycles

- Long run: interest in mechanical watches has grown since the 1990s.
- 2017 to early 2022: a boom in steel sports watches from Rolex, Patek Philippe and Audemars Piguet, with long waitlists and grey market premiums far above retail. Pre-owned indices peaked around the first quarter of 2022.
- 2022 onward: a significant correction. Many models fell back toward or below retail, especially outside the top references. Independents and select vintage remained strong.
- 2025 to 2026: auctions set records at the very top, including the record CHF 74.8 million Phillips Geneva sale in May 2026, while mid-market demand and US tariffs created uncertainty.
- Lesson: hype prices are not stable, and averages hide divergence between top-tier and ordinary pieces.

## 8. Watches as investments

Give facts, not recommendations.

- Watches are illiquid, carry high transaction costs, require insurance, servicing and secure storage, and are exposed to fakes and fashion.
- Returns are concentrated in a few references and top-condition examples. Survivorship bias is large: news coverage features record sales, not the many watches that lost value.
- Brands sometimes respond to speculation with price increases, production changes, or resale restrictions in purchase agreements.
- A sound framing: buy what you would enjoy wearing even if its value fell.
- For legal or tax questions about collecting, such as capital gains on collectibles, suggest a qualified advisor.

## 9. Buying safely: a checklist

1. Research the reference: correct dial, hands, bezel, caliber, serial range and bracelet for the claimed year.
2. Research the seller: reviews, history, physical address, and references from other buyers.
3. Ask for specific photos: dial in daylight and under a loupe, caseback, movement, lug holes, clasp codes, serial and reference engravings, the papers.
4. Ask for a timegrapher reading or video of the watch running.
5. Compare price to recent transaction prices, not asking prices. A price far below market is a warning.
6. Use protected payment: escrow, card payments, platform buyer protection, or in-person exchange at a dealer or bank. Avoid wire transfers, crypto and "friends and family" payments to strangers.
7. Check that the watch is not reported stolen, using registries such as The Watch Register where available, and that papers match.
8. Get an independent inspection soon after purchase if anything is doubtful, ideally within the return window.
9. Budget for an immediate service on vintage watches.

## 10. Common scams

- Too-good-to-be-true listings on marketplaces and social media.
- Payment redirection: fake escrow services or requests to pay outside the platform.
- Stolen watches sold with real papers.
- Bait and switch: photos of a genuine watch, a different one shipped.
- Superclones: high-quality fakes with cloned movements, now able to fool casual inspection. See `authentication.md`.
- Franken and redial watches described as original.
- Fake "AD allocation" or "waitlist placement" fees.
- Fraudulent inheritance or "estate sale" stories pushing urgency.

## 11. Ownership costs

- Service: mechanical watches need periodic service, often every five to ten years depending on brand guidance and condition. Chronographs and complications cost more. Brand service is often more expensive than independent service. Get current quotes.
- Parts: vintage parts can be scarce and expensive, especially dials, hands and bezel inserts.
- Insurance: specialist collectibles insurers or a home policy rider; keep receipts, photos and serial numbers.
- Straps, bracelets, polishing and battery changes add up over time.

## 12. Building a collection

- Decide on a theme: a brand, an era, a complication, tool watches, independents, Japanese watches, military watches.
- "Buy the seller": a trustworthy seller matters as much as the watch.
- Prioritize condition and originality over quantity.
- Keep a record of each watch: purchase details, serial, photos, service records and provenance.
- Consider the full cost of ownership before stretching a budget.
- Learn to read movements and dials with a loupe; the hobby's best protection is knowledge.
