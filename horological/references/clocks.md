## 1. Pendulum physics

- An ideal pendulum's period is T = 2π √(L/g), where L is length and g gravitational acceleration. Period depends on length, not on the weight of the bob.
- A "seconds pendulum," which takes one second per swing and two seconds for a full period, is about 0.994 m long at standard gravity. Longcase clocks typically use it.
- Circular error: wide swings take slightly longer than the ideal formula, which is why precision clocks use small arcs and why Huygens explored cycloidal cheeks.
- Temperature changes the pendulum's length. Compensation solutions:
  - Mercury pendulum, George Graham, 1721: a jar of mercury rises as the rod lengthens.
  - Gridiron pendulum, John Harrison, about 1726: alternating steel and brass rods cancel expansion.
  - Wood rods, which expand little along the grain.
  - Invar rods, after Guillaume's alloy, near-zero expansion.
- Air pressure and density also affect rate. Precision regulators sometimes run in sealed or low-pressure cases.
- Regulation: raising the bob with the rating nut makes the clock run faster; lowering it slows it.

## 2. Clock escapements

- Verge and foliot, then verge with pendulum: medieval to 17th century.
- Anchor or recoil: about 1670, the standard for domestic clocks.
- Deadbeat: George Graham, about 1715; no recoil, standard for precision regulators.
- Pin-wheel: French variant, robust.
- Grasshopper: John Harrison's nearly frictionless escapement, used in his wooden clocks and the modern Corpus Clock.
- Gravity escapement: impulse comes from small falling arms rather than the train, isolating the pendulum from train variations. Edmund Beckett Denison's double three-legged gravity escapement runs the Great Clock of Westminster.
- Riefler escapement: Sigmund Riefler, 1889, impulse through the pendulum suspension spring; used in observatory regulators.
- Free pendulum: the Shortt-Synchronome clock of 1921 used a master pendulum in a vacuum that did almost no work, with a slave clock doing the counting. Accurate to about a second per year, it was the best timekeeper until quartz.

## 3. Striking and chiming

- Count wheel or locking plate striking: a notched wheel controls the number of blows. If the strike gets out of sync, it must be corrected manually.
- Rack and snail striking: a stepped snail on the hour wheel sets the count each hour; self-correcting and able to repeat.
- Quarter chimes: Westminster quarters, originally composed for Great St Mary's in Cambridge in 1793 and adopted at Westminster in 1859; Whittington and St Michael's are other common chimes.
- Many striking clocks should not have their minute hand turned backward past the hour; advance the hands and let each strike complete, or follow the maker's instructions.

## 4. Types of clocks

| Type | Notes |
|---|---|
| Turret and tower clocks | Public timekeeping since the medieval period. |
| Lantern clocks | English brass weight-driven wall clocks of the 17th century. |
| Longcase or tall-case clocks | Seconds pendulum in a tall wooden case, from about 1670. "Grandfather clock" comes from Henry Clay Work's 1876 song "My Grandfather's Clock." |
| Bracket and mantel clocks | Spring-driven clocks for tables and mantelpieces. |
| Carriage clocks | Portable French and English clocks of the 19th century, descended from Breguet's pendule d'officier. Often with repeat and alarm. |
| Black Forest and cuckoo clocks | German wooden clocks from the 17th and 18th centuries. |
| Vienna regulators | Austrian precision wall clocks with long pendulums, 19th century. |
| Precision regulators | Observatory and workshop clocks, such as Riefler and Shortt-Synchronome. |
| Torsion or 400-day clocks | A rotating pendulum suspended on a thin wire, running a year per winding. Anton Harder's design of the 1880s; often called anniversary clocks. |
| Atmos | Jaeger-LeCoultre's perpetual clock, based on Jean-Léon Reutter's 1928 invention and made by Jaeger-LeCoultre from the 1930s. A sealed capsule of gas expands and contracts with temperature changes to wind the mainspring, and a slow torsion pendulum uses very little energy. A 1 degree Celsius change is commonly said to give about two days of running. |
| Mystery clocks | Hands appear to float on transparent dials, from Jean-Eugène Robert-Houdin's work and Cartier's famous pieces. |
| Rolling ball clocks | William Congreve's 1808 design, where a ball rolling on a zigzag track times the clock. |
| Synchronous electric clocks | Keep time by mains frequency, from Henry Warren's Telechron of the 1910s. |
| Quartz wall clocks | The modern default. |
| Radio-controlled clocks | Sync to national time signals; see `electronic.md`. |

## 5. Famous clocks

- Su Song's astronomical clock tower, China, about 1090.
- Prague astronomical clock or Orloj, 1410.
- Strasbourg astronomical clock: its present mechanism dates from 1843, by Jean-Baptiste Schwilgué, with a perpetual ecclesiastical computus.
- The Great Clock of Westminster, 1859: designed by Edmund Beckett Denison and built by Edward John Dent and Frederick Dent. "Big Ben" is the great bell, and the tower was renamed Elizabeth Tower in 2012.
- Jens Olsen's World Clock, Copenhagen City Hall, 1955.
- The Corpus Clock, Cambridge, 2008, with its "Chronophage" grasshopper.
- The Clock of the Long Now, a project to build a clock that runs for 10,000 years.

## 6. Marine chronometers and deck watches

- Marine chronometers are mounted in gimbals in a wooden box to stay level, use detent escapements and usually a fusee, and run two or eight days per winding.
- Navigators "rated" chronometers by recording their daily gain or loss, then applied the known rate rather than resetting them.
- Notable makers: John Arnold, Thomas Earnshaw, Thomas Mercer, Ulysse Nardin, Hamilton's Model 21 of WWII, and Soviet Poljot chronometers.
- Deck watches: precision pocket watches used to carry chronometer time to the deck for observations.
- Radio time signals from the early 20th century, and later satellite navigation, made marine chronometers obsolete for navigation.

## 7. Electric, quartz and atomic clocks

- Alexander Bain patented electric clocks in the 1840s.
- Master and slave systems synchronized clocks in factories, schools and railway stations.
- The first quartz clock: Warren Marrison and J. W. Horton at Bell Telephone Laboratories, 1927.
- Atomic clocks: the first, using ammonia, at the US National Bureau of Standards in 1949. The first accurate caesium clock: Louis Essen and Jack Parry at the UK National Physical Laboratory, 1955.
- Since 1967 the SI second is defined as 9,192,631,770 periods of the radiation from a transition of the caesium-133 atom.
- Optical lattice and ion clocks now exceed caesium clocks' accuracy by orders of magnitude, and a redefinition of the second is under discussion.
- GPS satellites carry atomic clocks, and positioning depends on their timing.

## 8. Time standards and time zones

- Before railways, towns kept local mean solar time. Railways needed standard time: Great Britain's railways adopted London time from the 1840s.
- North American railroads adopted standard time zones on 18 November 1883.
- 1884: the International Meridian Conference in Washington chose Greenwich as the prime meridian.
- GMT, Greenwich Mean Time, was the civil reference for decades. UTC, Coordinated Universal Time, has been the international basis of civil time since the 1960s, in its current form from 1972.
- TAI, International Atomic Time, is a pure atomic scale. UTC differs from TAI by leap seconds, inserted irregularly since 1972 to keep UTC near Earth's rotation. In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures decided to stop adding leap seconds by 2035.
- Daylight saving time was first adopted widely during WWI; rules vary by country and change often, which matters for world-time watches and smartwatches.

## 9. Clock care

- Pendulum clocks must be level and "in beat": the tick and tock should be evenly spaced. Adjust the crutch or case until they are.
- To move a pendulum clock, stop and remove the pendulum, secure or remove weights, and for longcase clocks lower the weights first.
- Do not oil a clock with household oil. Clocks need clock oil, applied sparingly to specific pivots, and a full overhaul every several years to a decade or so depending on use and condition.
- Wind regularly and fully but not forcefully. On weight-driven clocks, support the weights as you wind.
- Atmos clocks must be locked before moving, and should be kept level and away from drafts and direct sunlight.
- Keep clocks away from heat sources, which affect rate and dry lubricants.
