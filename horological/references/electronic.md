## 1. The electric and tuning-fork era

- Electric balance watches: a battery powers a coil that impulses a conventional balance. Hamilton Electric 500, January 1957, was the first sold; Lip and Elgin had shown prototypes in 1952. Contacts wear and the movements are temperamental; parts are scarce.
- Electronic balance watches: transistors replace contacts, as in the Lip R148, Timex Electric and Seiko EL-370.
- Tuning fork: Bulova Accutron, 1960, designed by Max Hetzel. A 360 Hz steel tuning fork drives a tiny index wheel through a jewel-tipped pawl. Accurate to about a minute a month, with a smooth sweeping seconds hand and an audible hum. The index wheel had 300 teeth and needed great precision to make. NASA used Accutron timers in spacecraft instruments. Repair requires specialists; adjusting the index mechanism is delicate.
- Bulova later revived the Accutron name, including a 2020 "Spaceview" with an electrostatic motor; the modern products are different technology from the original.

## 2. How a quartz watch works

1. A quartz crystal, cut as a tiny tuning fork, vibrates at 32,768 Hz when voltage is applied, because quartz is piezoelectric. 32,768 is 2 to the 15th power, so 15 binary divider stages produce exactly one pulse per second.
2. An integrated circuit counts the vibrations and sends a pulse to a stepper motor, usually a Lavet-type motor, once per second for analog displays.
3. The motor turns a gear train that drives the hands.

Details that matter:
- Crystals are trimmed at the factory, and most ICs apply digital inhibition, periodically dropping counts to correct for a crystal running slightly fast.
- Temperature changes crystal frequency. A crystal is most stable near wrist temperature, about 25 to 28 degrees Celsius, which is why standard quartz is about plus or minus 15 seconds per month.
- End-of-life indicator: many analog quartz watches signal a low battery by moving the seconds hand in two- or four-second jumps.
- Digital quartz watches use LED displays, from the 1970 to 1972 Pulsar, or LCDs, from 1972 onward.
- Ana-digi watches combine hands and digital displays.
- Meca-quartz: quartz timekeeping with a mechanical chronograph module, giving snappy pushers and a reset hand that snaps back. Examples: Seiko VK63 and VK64, Frederique Constant FC-292.

## 3. High-accuracy quartz

- Thermocompensation: the IC measures temperature several times a minute or more and corrects the count. This yields plus or minus 5 to 20 seconds per year.
- Notable high-accuracy quartz: Omega Megaquartz 2.4 MHz of 1970s; Seiko Twin Quartz 1978; Seiko 9F, from 1993, used in Grand Seiko, with a backlash-elimination system and instant date change, rated plus or minus 10 seconds per year; Breitling SuperQuartz, COSC certified; Longines VHP, first in 1984 and revived in 2017; Citizen Caliber 0100, 2019, an 8.4 MHz AT-cut crystal rated plus or minus 1 second per year.
- High-frequency crystals such as the Bulova Precisionist at 262.144 kHz give a smooth sweep, but frequency alone does not guarantee stability.
- AT-cut crystals vibrate in thickness shear mode and are far less temperature sensitive than standard tuning-fork crystals, but they consume more power.

## 4. Power sources: batteries, solar, kinetic

- Batteries: silver oxide button cells are typical for analog watches, identified by codes like SR626SW, also sold as 377. Lithium coin cells such as CR2016 power many digital watches. Expect two to five years from a typical analog quartz watch, longer for some lithium and low-power designs.
- Solar: a dial or ring acts as a photovoltaic cell and charges a capacitor or rechargeable lithium-ion cell. Citizen's Crystron Solar Cell of 1976 was early; the Eco-Drive name dates from 1995. Seiko Solar and Casio Tough Solar compete. A full charge can last months in the dark. The rechargeable cell eventually needs replacing, typically after a decade or more.
- Kinetic, originally AGS, 1988: a rotor drives a tiny generator that charges a capacitor or cell. Later versions add auto relay, which sleeps the hands to save energy. Seiko has largely retired Kinetic.
- Thermal: Citizen's Eco-Drive Thermo of 1999 generated power from body heat.

## 5. Spring Drive

Seiko's Spring Drive uses a mainspring and gear train like a mechanical watch, but replaces the escapement and balance with a regulator called the Tri-synchro Regulator:

1. The mainspring drives the train to a glide wheel, which spins at a nominal rate.
2. The spinning glide wheel generates a small electric current that powers a quartz oscillator and an IC. No battery is needed.
3. The IC compares the glide wheel speed with the quartz reference and applies an electromagnetic brake to hold it exactly on time.

Results: a perfectly smooth seconds hand, quartz-level accuracy of about plus or minus 15 seconds per month, or plus or minus 10 for some calibers, and long power reserves, typically 72 hours and eight days in the 9R01. The concept was proposed by Seiko engineer Yoshikazu Akahane in 1977; the first commercial Spring Drive appeared in 1999, and Grand Seiko adopted it in 2004. The 9RA2 and 9RA5 Spring Drive U.F.A. calibers, from 2024, are rated plus or minus 20 seconds per year.

Spring Drive needs service like a mechanical watch, because it has a mainspring and gear train with lubrication.

## 6. Radio-controlled and GPS time sync

Radio time signals:

| Station | Location | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| WWVB | Fort Collins, Colorado, US | 60 kHz |
| DCF77 | Mainflingen, Germany | 77.5 kHz |
| MSF | Anthorn, UK | 60 kHz |
| JJY | Two stations in Japan | 40 and 60 kHz |
| BPC | China | 68.5 kHz |

- Watches sync at night, when reception is best. Reception varies with distance, buildings, and orientation.
- Junghans Mega of 1990 was the first radio-controlled wristwatch.
- GPS: Citizen Satellite Wave, 2011, and Seiko Astron GPS Solar, 2012, receive time and in some cases location-based time zones from GPS satellites, working worldwide outdoors with a sky view.
- Bluetooth sync: Casio, Citizen and others sync with a phone app, which also updates time zone and daylight saving rules.
- Leap seconds: GPS-synced watches use the leap second count broadcast by the satellites. The international timekeeping community decided in 2022 to phase out leap seconds by 2035.

## 7. Smartwatches and hybrids

- Apple Watch, 2015, became the world's best-selling watch by unit volume. Samsung, Google Pixel, Garmin and others sell smartwatches and sports watches with GPS, heart rate and health sensors.
- Hybrid smartwatches keep analog hands with activity tracking and notifications, such as Withings and some Fossil and Frederique Constant models.
- Connected luxury: TAG Heuer Connected, Montblanc Summit, and Hublot's connected models.
- Smartwatches have reshaped the entry-level market far more than luxury mechanical watches.
- Lifespan is limited by battery degradation and software support, unlike mechanical watches.

## 8. Quartz servicing and failure modes

- Battery replacement should include checking and greasing the caseback gasket and, if water resistance matters, a pressure test.
- A dead battery left in a watch can leak and corrode the circuit. Replace promptly or remove it.
- Common faults: coil or circuit failure, worn train pivots, dried oil in the gear train, and corrosion from moisture.
- Cheap quartz movements, under about USD 20 wholesale, are usually replaced rather than repaired.
- High-end quartz, such as Grand Seiko 9F, Citizen 0100 and Breitling SuperQuartz, is designed to be serviced and the brands do so.
- Older analog quartz watches from the 1970s, such as Seiko Twin Quartz, Omega Beta 21 and Megaquartz, can be hard to repair because ICs and coils are no longer made.

## 9. Quartz movement makers

- Swiss: ETA, part of the Swatch Group; Ronda; ISA; Soprod for some models; Micro Crystal, a Swatch Group crystal maker.
- Japanese: Seiko Instruments and its TMI line, Epson, and Citizen's Miyota.
- Chinese makers supply very large volumes, often as clones of Japanese and Swiss designs.
