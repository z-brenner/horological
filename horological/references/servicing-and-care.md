## 1. Service intervals

- Modern brand guidance commonly ranges from about five to ten years for mechanical watches. Rolex currently suggests about ten years for its modern calibers; other brands vary. Check the brand's current recommendation.
- Service on condition, not only the calendar: falling amplitude, worsening rate, reduced power reserve, rough winding, moisture, or a failed pressure test.
- Water resistance should be tested more often than the movement is serviced if the watch is used in water, ideally annually.
- Quartz watches: battery changes every few years, with gasket checks; full service less often, mainly for the gear train and seals.
- Spring Drive: service like a mechanical watch.

## 2. What a full service includes

1. Inspection, timegrapher test and pressure test before work begins.
2. Removal of the movement, dial and hands.
3. Complete disassembly of the movement.
4. Ultrasonic cleaning of parts in specialized solutions.
5. Inspection and replacement of worn parts: mainspring, crown and stem, gaskets, sometimes balance staff, pivots or jewels.
6. Reassembly with several specific lubricants, and epilame treatment where required.
7. Regulation across positions and adjustment of beat error.
8. Casing with new gaskets, crown and pushers if needed.
9. Pressure testing for water resistance.
10. Optional case and bracelet refinishing.
11. Final testing over several days.

Ask for:
- A written estimate before work.
- Whether original parts replaced will be returned, especially dials, hands, crowns and bezels on vintage watches.
- No polishing, or light refinishing only, if originality matters.
- A warranty on the service, often one to two years.

## 3. Brand service versus independent watchmakers

| Brand service | Independent |
|---|---|
| Access to genuine parts and factory specifications | Can be faster and less expensive |
| Warranty on the service, often two years | Quality varies widely, from superb to poor |
| May replace original vintage parts with new service parts, hurting collectible value | Often better at preserving vintage originality |
| Standardized pricing, sometimes high | Parts access may be limited for some brands that restrict supply |
| Required for some warranty claims | Some brands certify independents through official service programs |

Many brands, including Swatch Group, Rolex and Richemont, have restricted parts supply to independent watchmakers to varying degrees. That can raise costs and limit options.

## 4. Finding a good watchmaker

- Training and certification: WOSTEP in Switzerland, the British Horological Institute in the UK, AWCI in the US with its CW21 certification, NAWCC for vintage and clocks, and brand-specific accreditations.
- Ask what equipment they use: timegrapher, pressure tester, ultrasonic cleaner, and whether they have parts accounts with the relevant brand.
- Ask for references and look for reviews on specialist forums.
- Ask them to quote a service for your specific caliber, and whether they will send it out.
- For vintage, ask about their policy on original parts and dials.

## 5. Troubleshooting by symptom

| Symptom | Most likely causes | First steps |
|---|---|---|
| Suddenly gains minutes per day | Magnetization; hairspring coils sticking | Compass test; demagnetize with an inexpensive demagnetizer; retest. If it persists, the hairspring may be tangled after a shock. |
| Gradually gains or loses more over months | Aging lubricants, wear, regulation drift | Rate test over several days; timegrapher; service if amplitude is low. |
| Stops overnight | Not enough wind; worn mainspring; high friction | Fully wind by hand and time the reserve. If it falls well short of spec, service. |
| Stops on the wrist, runs when shaken | Very low amplitude, dirty or worn movement, broken mainspring, failing automatic winding | Hand wind and test; service. |
| Automatic does not wind when worn | Low activity, worn reversers or rotor bearing, broken winding parts | Check if hand winding restores reserve; test on a winder; service. |
| Rotor noise or rubbing | Worn rotor bearing or loose screw | Stop wearing; service soon to prevent damage to the movement or caseback. |
| Crown spins freely, no winding | Broken stem, setting lever, or winding pinion | Service. |
| Date changes at noon | Time set 12 hours off | Advance the hands 12 hours. |
| Date changes slowly or partially | Creeping date by design, or a fault | Check the caliber; if instant date, service. |
| Moisture under the crystal | Failed gaskets, crown left open, or temperature shock | Stop the watch, remove the battery or keep it warm and dry, and get it to a watchmaker quickly. Rust spreads fast. |
| Chronograph hand does not reset to zero | Misaligned heart cam or hammer, or misset hand | Service; do not force the pusher. |
| Quartz seconds hand jumps every two or four seconds | End-of-life battery indicator | Replace the battery. |
| Quartz watch stopped after battery change | Bad battery, dirty contacts, or circuit or coil failure | Test with a new battery; service or movement replacement. |
| Hands misaligned at the hour | Hands not set correctly on their pinions | Watchmaker can reset; minor. |
| Loose bezel or bezel slipping | Worn bezel spring or click | Service or bezel replacement. |

## 6. What owners can safely do

- Change straps and size bracelets with the right tools, protecting the case from scratches.
- Demagnetize with a simple demagnetizer, following its instructions.
- Clean the exterior: a soft brush and mild soapy water for water-resistant watches with the crown screwed down, then rinse and dry. Leather straps should not be soaked.
- Polish an acrylic crystal with a plastic polish such as Polywatch.
- Measure rate at home and use positional resting. See `accuracy-and-timing.md`.
- Wind and set according to the manual.

Battery changes are possible at home on snap-back quartz watches, but risk damaging the gasket, the case and the movement, and lose water resistance without a pressure test. Recommend professional battery service for anything valuable or water resistant.

## 7. What owners should not do

- Open screw-down casebacks without proper tools and a pressure tester.
- Oil, adjust or regulate a movement without training.
- Polish a vintage case or bracelet.
- Clean or relume a dial, especially a radium dial.
- Force a crown, pusher, bezel or repeater slide.
- Set a calendar or moonphase during the forbidden window.
- Use ultrasonic jewelry cleaners on a cased watch.

## 8. Daily handling

- Wind manual watches at the same time each day, stopping when resistance increases. Do not force past the stop.
- Automatics can be hand wound to start; about 20 to 40 crown turns is typical. Check the caliber.
- Pull the crown out gently; unscrew screw-down crowns fully before pulling, and screw back down while gently pressing in to avoid cross-threading.
- Avoid setting the date between about 9 pm and 3 am on many movements. Set the time past 6 o'clock, set the date, then set the time.
- For most simple watches, setting the hands backward is acceptable. On calendars and complications, consult the manual.
- Remove the watch before heavy impacts: hammering, golf swings and some racquet sports can shock movements, although modern shock protection is good.

## 9. Water, sport, chemicals and temperature

- Rinse in fresh water after saltwater or chlorinated pool use, with the crown screwed down.
- Do not operate pushers or crowns underwater unless the watch is rated for it.
- Avoid hot tubs, saunas, steam rooms and hot showers.
- Keep perfumes, sunscreen and solvents off straps and gaskets.
- Avoid extreme temperature shocks, such as jumping from a hot deck into cold water.

## 10. Storage and winders

- Store in a dry place away from magnets and direct sunlight. Silica gel in watch boxes helps in humid climates.
- Winders keep automatics running, useful for calendars and moonphases. Set the turns per day and direction to the movement's needs; overwinding is not a risk for automatics with slipping bridles, but constant running does add wear.
- Letting a watch stop is not harmful. Whether oils age differently in a stopped watch than a running one is debated among watchmakers, and neither choice is a meaningful risk for a normal owner.
- Remove batteries from quartz watches stored for long periods.
- Keep Eco-Drive and solar watches exposed to light periodically.

## 11. Vintage-specific care

- Treat any vintage watch as not water resistant unless tested recently.
- Get a service soon after purchase unless one is documented; old oil can cause wear.
- Keep original parts if any are replaced.
- Radium dials, typically pre-1960s: do not open, scrape, or inhale; keep away from children's handling; store away from living areas in quantity. For collections, consult a radiation professional.
- Tritium dials are low hazard in normal handling but should not be disturbed.
- Avoid ultraviolet exposure if a tropical or patinated dial's current state is valued, though fading is unpredictable.

## 12. Warranties

- Brand warranties commonly run two to five years, with some longer, such as Oris's ten years on certain calibers. Rolex, Tudor and Omega offer five years on many current models. Terms change; check the brand.
- Warranties usually require purchase from an authorized dealer and exclude damage, water ingress from misuse, and third-party repairs.
- Grey market purchases may lack brand warranty or have a shortened one.
- Certified pre-owned programs offer their own warranties.
