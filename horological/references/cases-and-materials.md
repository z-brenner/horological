## 1. Case anatomy

- Bezel: the ring around the crystal. Fixed, rotating unidirectional, bidirectional, or internal.
- Mid-case or case band: the central body, which usually carries the lugs.
- Caseback: screw-down, snap-on, screwed with small screws, or display with a sapphire window.
- Lugs or horns: the projections holding the strap. Drilled lugs, with holes through, make strap changes easier and are prized on vintage pieces.
- Crown and tube: the crown screws onto or pushes against a tube fixed in the case. Gaskets inside the crown seal it. Screw-down crowns compress a gasket for higher water resistance.
- Crown guards or shoulders: protect the crown.
- Pushers: chronograph or function buttons. Screw-down pushers are needed for genuine underwater use.
- Rehaut or flange: the inner ring between dial and crystal, often printed with a scale or engraved, as on post-2007 Rolex.
- Gaskets: nitrile, Viton, or Teflon rings at the crystal, caseback and crown.

## 2. Dimensions and wearability

- Diameter is measured across the case excluding the crown. Brands differ on whether they include the bezel.
- Lug-to-lug, the tip-to-tip length, decides how a watch wears more than diameter does. If the lugs extend beyond the flat top of the wrist, the watch overhangs.
- Thickness includes the crystal. Domed crystals can add a millimeter or more.
- Lug width determines strap size, usually 18, 19, 20, 21 or 22 mm.
- Integrated bracelets, sloping lugs and curved casebacks wear smaller than the numbers suggest.
- Historical sizing: men's watches were typically 30 to 36 mm until the 1990s, then grew through the 2000s to 40 to 44 mm, and have since trended back toward 36 to 40 mm.
- Wearability is personal. Always suggest checking lug-to-lug against the user's flat wrist width.

## 3. Case construction types

- Three-piece: bezel, mid-case and caseback.
- Monocoque: one-piece case loaded from the front, sealing through the crystal and bezel, as in many 1960s and 1970s dive watches and the original Royal Oak.
- Oyster: Rolex's screw-down case with screwed caseback, crown and bezel construction.
- Compressor: water pressure pushes the caseback seal tighter. Ervin Piquerez SA, or EPSA, made compressor cases for many brands in the 1950s to 1970s, often with internal rotating bezels and two crowns.
- Cushion, tonneau, rectangular or tank, and asymmetric shapes.
- Integrated bracelet cases, from the Royal Oak and Nautilus onward.
- Hooded lugs, C-cases, and "lyre" lugs are vintage design features.

## 4. Case and bracelet materials

| Material | Notes |
|---|---|
| 316L stainless steel | The standard: corrosion resistant, easily machined and polished. |
| 904L stainless steel | More nickel and molybdenum, better corrosion resistance, harder to machine. Rolex adopted it starting in 1985 and calls it Oystersteel. |
| Titanium grade 2 | Commercially pure, light, softer, scratches easily. |
| Titanium grade 5 | Ti-6Al-4V alloy, harder and takes a better polish. |
| Hardened titanium | Proprietary surface or alloy hardening, such as Grand Seiko Brilliant Hard Titanium and Citizen Super Titanium with Duratect. |
| Bronze | Tin bronze or aluminum bronze; develops patina. Can stain skin; often paired with a steel or titanium caseback. |
| Yellow, rose and white gold | 18k is 75 percent gold. 14k is 58.5 percent. Rose gold uses copper, white gold uses palladium or nickel and is often rhodium plated. |
| Proprietary gold alloys | Rolex Everose, 2005, and Omega Sedna, 2013, add platinum or palladium so rose gold does not fade. Hublot Magic Gold is a scratch-resistant gold ceramic composite. |
| Platinum | Usually 950, very dense and heavy, scratches but does not lose material easily. Rolex reserves an ice-blue dial for platinum models. |
| Ceramic | Zirconium oxide, scratch resistant, colorfast; can crack or shatter if struck. Rolex Cerachrom bezels arrived in 2005. Rado pioneered ceramic watches. |
| Carbon composites | Forged carbon and thin-ply carbon, very light, each case pattern unique. |
| Sapphire cases | Machined from synthetic corundum, transparent and extremely expensive. |
| Tantalum, palladium, Damascus steel, meteorite | Niche materials. |

Use `horocalc.py gold` to convert karat and fineness.

## 5. Surface finishing and coatings

- Satin or brushed: fine parallel grain, hides scratches.
- Mirror polished: shows every scratch.
- Zaratsu or blade polishing: a Seiko technique using a rotating tin plate to give distortion-free mirror surfaces with sharp edges.
- Bead blasting or sandblasting: matte, tool-watch look.
- Polishing a case removes metal. Sharp chamfers and original brushing are highly valued on vintage watches, and overpolishing destroys value. Ask for "no polish" at service unless the owner wants it.
- Coatings: PVD and DLC, diamond-like carbon, give black or colored finishes and some scratch resistance. Coatings can scratch through to the base metal and are hard to refinish.
- Surface hardening: Sinn Tegiment and Damasko processes harden steel cases deeply.

## 6. Crystals

| Crystal | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| Acrylic, plexi, Hesalite | Cheap, flexible, scratches polish out with Polywatch, does not shatter into shards | Scratches easily |
| Mineral glass, Seiko Hardlex | Cheaper than sapphire, more scratch resistant than acrylic | Scratches eventually, can shatter |
| Sapphire | About 9 on the Mohs scale, scratch resistant | Can chip at edges, reflects without anti-reflective coating |

- Anti-reflective coating can be on the inside, outside, or both. Outer coatings scratch and show as patches.
- Domed and box crystals are popular on vintage-inspired designs.
- Omega's Hesalite on the Speedmaster was retained for spaceflight because it cannot shatter into fragments in zero gravity.
- Rolex introduced sapphire on most models from the late 1970s to 1980s.

## 7. Dials

Construction and finishes:
- Brass or silver base plates with printed, applied, or stamped markers.
- Grand feu enamel: powdered glass fired repeatedly; extremely durable and expensive. Champlevé, cloisonné and miniature painting are decorative enamel techniques.
- Guilloché: engine-turned patterns cut by a rose engine, by hand or machine.
- Sunburst, lacquer, galvanic, gilt, meteorite, stone dials such as onyx and lapis, aventurine, mother-of-pearl, urushi lacquer, porcelain.
- Gilt dials: gold-colored printing on glossy black, made by galvanic processes, common on Rolex tool watches until the mid 1960s.
- Matte dials replaced gilt in the 1960s.

Layouts and designs:
- Sector dials: concentric rings dividing minutes and hours, popular in the 1930s.
- California dials: Roman numerals on the top half and Arabic on the bottom.
- Pie-pan dials: dial edges angled down like a pie tin, as on Omega Constellations.
- Sandwich dials: a cutout top plate over a lume layer, a Panerai signature.
- Tropical dials: blacks that faded to brown through UV and chemical change, prized when even.
- Ghost bezels: faded bezel inserts.
- Applied indices are separate pieces attached to the dial. Printed indices are painted. Lume plots are separate dots of luminous material.

## 8. Hands

- Baton, sword, dauphine, leaf, alpha, syringe, cathedral, Breguet or pomme, spade, arrow, Mercedes on Rolex tool watches, snowflake on Tudor divers, broad arrow on the Speedmaster and Seamaster 300, lollipop or ball seconds hands.
- Blued steel hands: heat-blued for color and corrosion resistance; prized.
- Replaced or service hands are common on vintage watches and change value.

## 9. Luminous materials

| Material | Era | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Radium paint | about 1910s to 1960s | Radioactive alpha emitter; the "Radium Girls" scandal of the 1920s. The paint's phosphor burns out and no longer glows, but the radium remains radioactive with a half-life of about 1,600 years. Do not open, scrape, sand or inhale. Store vintage radium watches without daily skin contact if concerned, and consult a radiation professional for collections. |
| Promethium | 1960s and 1970s, limited use | Short half-life, rare. |
| Tritium paint | about 1960s to late 1990s | Weak beta emitter, half-life 12.3 years, so vintage tritium no longer glows meaningfully. Dial marks like "T SWISS T" or "SWISS T < 25" indicate tritium below 25 millicuries. "T < 25" and "SWISS - T < 25" variants help date dials. |
| LumiNova | from 1993, Nemoto | Non-radioactive strontium aluminate; charges with light. |
| Super-LumiNova | late 1990s onward, RC Tritec | Licensed version of LumiNova; grades such as C3 green and BGW9 blue. |
| Lumibrite | Seiko | Seiko's photoluminescent compound. |
| Chromalight | Rolex, from 2008 | Blue-emitting. |
| Tritium gas tubes, GTLS | 1990s onward | Sealed borosilicate tubes glow continuously for years, dimming with tritium's half-life. Ball, Luminox, Marathon, Traser and mb-microtec. |

Relumed hands and dials can be detected by color, texture and mismatch between hands and dial. See `authentication.md`.

## 10. Water resistance

Ratings indicate a static pressure test at the factory, not a safe dive depth.

| Marking | Practical meaning |
|---|---|
| Water resistant, or 3 ATM, 30 m | Splashes, rain and hand washing. Not swimming. |
| 5 ATM, 50 m | Light swimming at the surface; many brands still advise against it. |
| 10 ATM, 100 m | Swimming and snorkeling. |
| 20 ATM, 200 m and up | Recreational scuba diving. |
| ISO 6425 diver | Tested to the standard; see below. |

- 1 bar is about 10 meters of seawater. Use `horocalc.py water` to convert units.
- Water resistance is a condition, not a permanent property. Gaskets dry, crack and lose elasticity. Test annually if you swim with the watch, and at every service.
- Never operate a non-screw-down crown or pushers underwater. Make sure screw-down crowns are fully screwed in.
- Avoid hot tubs, saunas, steam rooms and hot showers: heat expands gaskets and air, and temperature shocks draw in moisture.
- Moisture under the crystal needs prompt attention; remove the battery or stop the watch and see a watchmaker.

ISO 6425, divers' watches, main requirements:
- Minimum rating of 100 m.
- Tested to 125 percent of the rated pressure.
- A time preselection device, such as a unidirectional rotating bezel, marked to show elapsed dive time. Check the current edition for exact marking rules.
- Legibility at 25 cm in total darkness, including an indication that the watch is running.
- Resistance to magnetism, shock, salt water, and thermal shock, and secure strap attachment.
- A "saturation diving" class adds helium tests for watches marked for mixed-gas diving.
- Each watch is tested individually rather than by sample.
- A watch only conforms if the maker has tested it to the standard; "DIVER'S" on the dial is the conventional marking.

ISO 22810 covers general water-resistant watches.

Helium escape valves let helium that has diffused into the watch during saturation diving escape during decompression. Rolex and Doxa are often credited with the 1967 development for COMEX; many brands since. Recreational divers do not need one.

## 11. Magnetic shielding

- Soft iron inner cases, or Faraday cages, around the movement: IWC Ingenieur from 1955, Rolex Milgauss from 1956, Sinn and others.
- Non-magnetic materials: silicon hairsprings and escapements, Rolex Parachrom, Omega's Master Chronometer architecture resisting 15,000 gauss without a cage.
- Daily sources of magnetism: phone speakers, laptop and tablet magnets, magnetic bag clasps, induction cooktops, fridge door seals and MRI rooms. See `standards.md` for ISO 764 and `horocalc.py magnet` for unit conversions.

## 12. Bracelets, straps and clasps

Bracelet types:
- Oyster: three-link, flat, Rolex since the late 1940s.
- Jubilee: five-link, dressier, Rolex 1945 for the Datejust.
- President: semi-circular three-link, for Day-Date and gold Datejusts.
- Beads of rice: Omega's many-link vintage style.
- H-link, flat-link, Milanese mesh, shark mesh, Engineer, and integrated bracelets.

Bracelet construction:
- Solid versus hollow or folded links: solid links are heavier and more durable.
- Solid versus folded end links: folded end links rattle and are a vintage-era cost saving.
- Tapering bracelets, which narrow toward the clasp, are more comfortable and elegant.

Clasps:
- Fold-over with safety lock, butterfly deployant, and tool-free micro-adjustment systems such as Rolex Glidelock, Easylink, Tudor T-fit, Omega's comfort setting, and Seiko's diver extension.

Straps:
- Leather: calf, alligator, shell cordovan, suede. Alligator and some exotic leathers require CITES paperwork for export to some jurisdictions.
- Rubber and FKM fluoroelastomer: best for water.
- NATO and Zulu straps: single-piece nylon straps that pass under the case, so the watch stays on if a spring bar fails.
- Spring bars and quick-release bars: check the diameter and fit before swapping.
