## 1. What to ask the user for

Ask once, as a list:

- Clear, well-lit photos of the dial, straight on and at an angle.
- The caseback, outside and inside if it can be opened safely by a professional.
- The movement, including any engravings on the bridges and under the balance if visible.
- Any numbers between the lugs, on the case sides, or on the rehaut.
- The crown, the clasp and any codes stamped inside it.
- Case diameter, lug width and thickness.
- Papers, receipts, service records, and how the owner acquired it.

Warn the user not to open screw-down casebacks without the right tools; damaged backs and gaskets are expensive.

## 2. Where the numbers are

| Number | Typical location |
|---|---|
| Reference or model number | Caseback, between the lugs, inside the caseback, or on papers |
| Case serial | Caseback, between the lugs, on the case side, or on the rehaut |
| Movement serial | On a bridge or the main plate |
| Caliber number | On the main plate under the dial, under the balance, or on a bridge |
| Dial code | Printed at the bottom of some dials, such as Seiko's codes near 6 o'clock |
| Clasp or bracelet code | Inside the clasp or on end links |
| Hallmarks and assay marks | Inside the caseback or on the case |

## 3. Dating by brand

Use these as starting points and confirm with archives or specialist databases.

### Rolex
- The reference number is engraved between the lugs at 12 o'clock and the serial between the lugs at 6, under the bracelet.
- Numeric serials rose to about 9,999,999 around 1987.
- Letter-prefix serials followed, approximately: R about 1987 to 1988, L 1988 to 1990, E 1990 to 1991, X and N 1991, C 1992, S 1993, W 1994 to 1995, T 1996, U 1997, A 1998 to 1999, P 2000, K 2001, Y 2002, F 2003 to 2004, D 2005, Z 2006, M 2007 to 2008, V 2008 to 2009, G 2010. Overlaps are normal.
- Random alphanumeric serials began around 2010 and cannot be dated from the serial alone.
- Serials moved to the rehaut engraving from about 2005 to 2008; the rehaut also carries ROLEX ROLEX ROLEX engraving.
- Vintage casebacks are often stamped inside with a quarter and year, such as "II.67" for the second quarter of 1967.
- Clasps carry date codes in some periods.
- Model-specific details such as dial "marks," bezel fonts and hands identify sub-generations. Specialist sites like Jake's Rolex World and Rolex Passion Report document them.

### Omega
- Movement serial numbers date movement production; cases were sometimes assembled later.
- Omega's online vintage database lists references and calibers, and the paid Extract of the Archives confirms production date and original destination.
- Case references such as 145.022-69 include a suffix that often relates to the case design year rather than the production year.
- Logo and font changes help: the shape of the Omega symbol and dial script changed across eras.

### Patek Philippe
- Movement and case numbers are recorded in the archives. Patek's paid Extract from the Archives gives manufacture and sale dates and original configuration.
- Patek has published broad serial ranges by year, and specialist books such as the Patek Philippe reference works cover them.

### Seiko and Grand Seiko
- The caseback serial's first digit is the last digit of the production year. The second character is the month: 1 to 9 for January to September, then O, N and D for October, November and December. The remaining digits are a production count.
- The decade must be inferred from the caliber's production period, the case design, and the dial. Run `horocalc.py seiko-serial` with the serial and, if known, the caliber's production years.
- Model codes, such as 6105-8110, combine the caliber, 6105, and the case number, 8110.
- Japanese domestic market models often carry Japanese day wheels or kanji, and "Made in Japan" dial text.

### Citizen
- Many Citizen casebacks carry a serial whose first digit is the last digit of the year. Confirm with the caliber's known production dates.

### Longines
- Movement serials are recorded, and Longines' heritage department has historically issued archive extracts on request. Longines serial ranges by year are widely published.

### Heuer
- Case serial numbers and reference numbers; OnTheDash is the reference site for vintage Heuer chronographs.

### Universal Genève, Zenith, IWC, Jaeger-LeCoultre, Vacheron Constantin, Audemars Piguet
- Most of these houses keep archives and issue extracts for a fee. Zenith and IWC have long-running heritage services. Check each brand's site for current procedures.

### American makers
- Waltham, Elgin, Hamilton, Illinois, Hampden, Howard and others: serial numbers on the movement date production precisely. Use pocketwatchdatabase.com and NAWCC resources.

### Swatch
- Swatch watches typically carry a date code on the back or packaging indicating the season and year.

### Casio
- The module number, stamped on the caseback and visible inside, identifies the movement. Many casebacks also carry a serial whose first digits relate to production date. Verify with collector resources.

## 4. Reading the dial

- Brand logos, coronets, fonts and the position of text changed over time and identify eras.
- "Swiss Made," "Swiss," "T Swiss Made T," "Swiss T < 25," "Swiss Made" circled in a lume plot, and similar markings indicate lume type and era. See `cases-and-materials.md`.
- Chronometer text: "Superlative Chronometer Officially Certified," "Chronometer," "Officially Certified."
- Lume color and texture: radium often darkens and burns the dial around plots; tritium ages to cream, yellow or brown; LumiNova stays white or green and glows brightly.
- Printing: gilt versus matte, printed versus applied markers, crisp versus blobby edges. Compare with known genuine examples.
- Redials: fonts slightly off, misaligned text, missing details such as the tiny printing under 6 o'clock, wrong lume color. See `authentication.md`.

## 5. Reading the movement

- Look for engraved caliber numbers on the main plate or under the balance, and maker logos. Historic ébauche makers include ETA, A. Schild or AS, Felsa, FHF or Fontainemelon, Peseux, Unitas, Valjoux, Venus, Landeron, Lemania and Enicar.
- Bridge shapes and layouts identify calibers. Compare with Ranfft's movement archive and Caliber Corner.
- Jewel counts, regulator types, shock protection, finishing patterns and rotor shapes help narrow down a version.
- A movement that does not match the reference's correct caliber is a red flag for a franken or a replacement.

## 6. Reading the case and bracelet

- Case shape, crown style, crown guards, lug drilling and caseback engraving changed over time.
- Gold hallmarks can date a case, especially British assay marks with date letters.
- Bracelet clasp codes, end link numbers and bracelet styles indicate eras. A wrong bracelet is common and lowers value but is easily corrected.
- Crystal type: acrylic on most watches before the late 1970s to 1980s, sapphire later.

## 7. Pocket watches

- American sizes follow the Lancashire gauge: size 0 is 1 5/30 inches across the pillar plate, and each size adds 1/30 of an inch, so size 18 is 1 23/30 inches, about 44.9 mm, and size 16 is 1 21/30 inches, about 43.2 mm. Railroad watches were 16 or 18 size.
- Case types: hunter, with a cover over the dial, or open face. Railroad-grade watches were open face, lever set.
- Setting: pendant set, through the crown, or lever set, by pulling out a lever under the bezel. Railroad standards required lever setting to avoid accidental time changes.
- Cases and movements were often sold separately, so the case may not match the movement's date or maker.
- Fusee, verge, cylinder and lever escapements help date European watches.

## 8. Military watches

- US military watches often carry specification numbers, contract numbers and NSN stock numbers on the caseback.
- British issued watches carry the broad arrow mark, NATO stock numbers such as 6645-99 prefixes, and issue numbers.
- German WWII B-Uhren carry FL 23883 markings and maker identification.
- Fakes and "fantasy" military watches are common, and genuine issued cases are sometimes fitted with incorrect dials. Check issue records and specialist forums.

## 9. When the trail runs cold

- Search the brand name on Mikrolisk to identify obscure trademarks and makers.
- Post clear photos on a specialist forum for that brand or category.
- Contact the brand's heritage department.
- Consult a specialist auction house; many will give informal opinions on consigned pieces.
- State clearly what the evidence supports, what it does not, and what single piece of information would settle it.
