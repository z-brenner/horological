# Complications

A complication is any function beyond hours, minutes and seconds. This file explains how each works, the variants that matter, and what to look for.

## 1. Chronographs

A chronograph is a stopwatch built into a watch. The seconds hand of a chronograph usually runs only when started; the watch's own running seconds sit in a subdial.

Layouts:
- Mono-pusher: one button cycles start, stop and reset, often through the crown. Early and elegant.
- Two-pusher: start/stop at 2 and reset at 4. The norm since the 1930s.
- Bi-compax: two subdials, typically running seconds and a 30-minute counter.
- Tri-compax: three subdials adding a 12-hour counter. Valjoux 7750 layouts put them at 6, 9 and 12.

Control mechanism:
- Column wheel: a castellated wheel that rotates in steps to coordinate the levers. Smooth, precise pusher feel, traditionally a mark of quality. Examples: Valjoux 72, Lemania 2310, Zenith El Primero, Rolex 4130.
- Cam or shuttle: a heart-shaped or stepped cam with levers. Cheaper, robust, firmer pushers. Example: Valjoux 7750, Omega 861.

Coupling or clutch:
- Horizontal or lateral clutch: a coupling wheel swings into mesh with the chronograph runner. The seconds hand can stutter or jump on start, and running the chronograph continuously adds wear and drains amplitude. Classic and visible through a display back.
- Vertical clutch: friction discs couple the chronograph runner to the fourth wheel. No start jump, can run continuously as a running seconds. Examples: Seiko 6139, Rolex 4130, Patek CH 29-535 PS.
- Swinging pinion or oscillating pinion: Heuer and Breitling's Calibre 11 and the Calibre 12, compact and inexpensive.

Construction:
- Integrated: chronograph designed into the base caliber.
- Modular: a module stacked on a base movement. Often thicker, sometimes less smooth, easier to make. Dubois-Dépraz modules are the classic example.

Variants:
- Flyback or retour en vol: one push of the reset button while running resets and restarts the hand. Developed for pilots.
- Rattrapante or split-seconds: a second chronograph hand can be stopped independently for intermediate times, then rejoined. Difficult to build. The double rattrapante, such as the A. Lange & Söhne Double Split, also splits the minutes.
- Foudroyante or jumping seconds: a hand that jumps in fractions of a second, such as eighths.
- High-frequency: Zenith's Defy El Primero 21 measures hundredths of a second with a separate 50 Hz chronograph escapement. TAG Heuer's Mikrograph and Mikrogirder pushed further.
- Mono-counter, 15-minute and regatta countdown chronographs.

## 2. Timing scales and how to use them

| Scale | Purpose | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| Tachymeter | Speed over a known distance, usually one km or mile | Speed equals 3,600 divided by elapsed seconds. 30 seconds over a mile means 120 mph. Only useful for times under 60 seconds on a standard scale. |
| Telemeter | Distance from an event seen and then heard | Uses the speed of sound, about 343 m/s at 20 degrees Celsius. Start at the flash, stop at the sound. |
| Pulsometer | Heart rate | Graduated for a set number of beats, often 15 or 30. Count that many pulses and read the scale. |
| Asthmometer | Breathing rate | Medical, rare. |
| Decimal or 1/100 minute | Industrial timing | Used by engineers and time-and-motion studies. |
| Regatta | Sailing race starts | Countdown, usually five or ten minutes. |

## 3. Time zone complications

- GMT, true or "flyer" type: the local hour hand jumps in one-hour steps without stopping the watch, while the 24-hour hand keeps home time. Best for travelers. Examples: Rolex 3285, Tudor MT5652, Grand Seiko 9S86. The date usually follows local time.
- GMT, "caller" or "office" type: the 24-hour hand jumps independently and the local hour hand is set with the minutes. Best for tracking a second zone from home. Examples: ETA 2893, Sellita SW330, many affordable GMTs. On a caller GMT, changing the local time moves the date only when you set the main hands through midnight.
- Dual time: a second 12-hour subdial or second hour hand, without a 24-hour scale. Cannot distinguish day from night unless there is an indicator.
- World timer: a ring of city names and a rotating 24-hour disc show all zones at once. Louis Cottier's 1930s design is the classic, used in the Patek Philippe 1415 and 2523. Modern one-push world timers advance the city and hour together, such as Patek's patented system introduced in the 5110 and carried into its successors. Note that city names and offsets age: half-hour and quarter-hour zones like India, Nepal and parts of Australia need special handling, and zone changes, daylight saving time and city renaming make vintage world timers out of date.
- 24-hour watches: a single hand rotates once per day, from Glycine Airman to Russian Raketa 24-hour models.

## 4. Calendars

| Type | What it does | Corrections needed |
|---|---|---|
| Simple date | Date only, 31-day cycle | Five times a year, at the end of each short month. |
| Day-date | Adds the day of the week | Same as simple date. |
| Triple or complete calendar | Day, date and month, often with moonphase | Same as simple date. |
| Annual calendar | Knows 30- and 31-day months | Once a year, at the end of February. Patek Philippe 5035 of 1996 was the first. |
| Perpetual calendar | Knows months and the four-year leap cycle | None until a skipped Gregorian leap year: 2100, 2200 and 2300 are not leap years; 2000 was. |
| Secular perpetual | Also knows the century rule | None for centuries. Very rare. |

How a perpetual calendar works: a program wheel, or 48-month cam, turns once every four years. Its notches have depths corresponding to month lengths, with one notch for February in a leap year. A feeler reads the depth at month-end and advances the date the right number of steps. A grand lever system then advances month, day and leap-year indications.

Practical notes:
- Do not set a calendar through the change window, typically late evening to early morning. The forbidden window varies by caliber; check the manual.
- If a perpetual calendar stops, it must be advanced, often using recessed correctors with a stylus, in a set order. Many owners use a winder for this reason.
- Some modern perpetuals, such as IWC's Kurt Klaus design of 1985, allow all indications to be advanced through the crown.
- Equation of time: shows the difference between apparent solar time and mean time, which varies from about minus 14 to plus 16 minutes over the year. A "marching" equation shows solar time with a separate hand.

## 5. Moonphase and astronomical displays

- The synodic month, new moon to new moon, averages 29.530589 days.
- A standard moon disc has 59 teeth, two moons, advanced one tooth per day, so it models 29.5 days. Error: about 0.0306 days per lunation, which accumulates to one day roughly every 2.6 years.
- The common 135-tooth precision disc, driven through a reduction gear, models about 29.53125 days, accurate to one day in roughly 122 years. Some modern designs claim one day in hundreds or even thousands of years.
- Use `horocalc.py moonphase` for exact drift figures.
- Setting a moonphase: find the date of the last full moon, rotate the disc to full, then advance one click per day since.
- Southern hemisphere moonphases show the crescent reversed.
- Other astronomical displays: sidereal time, sky charts such as Patek's Celestial, tide indicators, sunrise and sunset, seasons, zodiac, Chinese calendar and Islamic Hijri calendar displays.

## 6. Chiming complications

| Complication | Behavior |
|---|---|
| Quarter repeater | On demand, strikes hours then quarters. |
| Five-minute repeater | Hours then five-minute intervals. |
| Minute repeater | Hours on a low gong, quarters as double strikes, then minutes on a high gong. |
| Decimal repeater | Hours, ten-minute intervals, minutes. Lange Zeitwerk Minute Repeater uses this. |
| Grande sonnerie | Strikes hours and quarters automatically every quarter, and can repeat on demand. |
| Petite sonnerie | Automatically strikes hours at the hour and quarters at the quarters, without repeating the hour each quarter. |
| Carillon | Three or more hammers and gongs, for a melodic strike. |

Engineering points:
- A slide or pusher arms a separate mainspring or rack. Snails, cams cut in steps, read the current hour, quarter and minute, and racks transfer the counts to the hammers.
- A governor regulates the strike speed. Anchor governors buzz audibly; centrifugal or inertial governors are silent and preferred.
- Gongs are hardened steel wires around the movement. Cathedral gongs circle the movement more than once for longer resonance.
- Sound depends on case material and construction. Gold is warm and quiet; platinum is dull; steel and titanium are often louder. Patek's 6300A Grandmaster Chime in steel was sold partly for that reason.
- Never operate a repeater's slide while the hands are being set, and never force a slide that resists. Repairs are expensive.

## 7. Alarms

- Mechanical wristwatch alarms use a separate barrel and a hammer striking a pin or the caseback.
- Classics: Vulcain Cricket, 1947, famous for presidential owners; Jaeger-LeCoultre Memovox, 1950, automatic from 1956; Seiko Bell-Matic; Omega Memomatic.
- Modern examples include the Breguet Réveil du Tsar and the Jaeger-LeCoultre Master Memovox. Mechanical alarms are rare today, so verify any claimed alarm function against the caliber specification.

## 8. Tourbillons, carrousels and rotating escapements

- Tourbillon: the escapement and balance ride in a rotating cage, usually turning once per minute, so gravity's positional errors average out when the watch is held vertically, as a pocket watch is. Breguet patented it in 1801.
- In a wristwatch, which constantly changes orientation, the accuracy benefit is small and disputed. Tourbillons are primarily demonstrations of skill and craft.
- Flying tourbillon: the cage is supported from one side only, with no bridge on the dial side. Credited to Alfred Helwig of Glashütte, 1920.
- Multi-axis tourbillons: cages inclined or rotating on two or three axes, such as Jaeger-LeCoultre Gyrotourbillon of 2004, Greubel Forsey's inclined double tourbillon, and Thomas Prescher's triple-axis designs.
- Carrousel or karrusel: patented by Bahne Bonniksen in 1892. The escapement rotates, but the cage is driven differently, with the fourth wheel not fixed at the cage center. Blancpain revived it.
- Tourbillon prices fell sharply in the 2010s, with Chinese-made tourbillons and Swiss brands like TAG Heuer and Frederique Constant offering them at much lower prices. Price no longer signals quality; finishing and regulation do.

## 9. Constant force and power management

- Fusee and chain: a cone-shaped pulley gives low leverage at full wind and high leverage when nearly unwound, evening out torque. A. Lange & Söhne's Tourbillon Pour le Mérite revived it in a wristwatch in 1994.
- Remontoir d'égalité: a small secondary spring is rewound at intervals by the mainspring and delivers constant energy to the escapement. Often visible as a jumping seconds.
- Constant force escapements, such as Girard-Perregaux's silicon blade design.
- Power reserve indicator: a differential gear compares the winding side of the barrel with the unwinding side, driving a hand that shows remaining reserve.
- Stop-work: a mechanism, often a Maltese cross, that uses only the middle part of the spring.

## 10. Alternative time displays

- Regulator: separate hands for hours, minutes and seconds on different axes, from precision clocks where the minute hand dominated.
- Jumping hour: the hour appears in a window and jumps instantly at the top of the hour.
- Wandering hour or satellite hours: hour numerals ride on rotating discs that sweep across a minute arc. Audemars Piguet's 1990s Star Wheel and Urwerk are examples, with roots in 17th century night clocks.
- Retrograde: a hand sweeps an arc and snaps back to zero. Used for minutes, dates or days.
- Digital mechanical: A. Lange & Söhne Zeitwerk of 2009 uses jumping discs for hours and minutes, with a remontoir.
- Dead-beat seconds or seconde morte: a mechanical seconds hand that ticks once per second like quartz, used in precision observation watches.
- 24-hour, decimal and single-hand displays. Single-hand watches like Meistersinger read time to about five minutes.
- Sector and California dials are dial designs, not complications. See `glossary.md`.

## 11. Complication hierarchy and "grand complication"

- Simple complications: date, small seconds, power reserve.
- Moderate: chronograph, GMT, annual calendar, moonphase.
- High: perpetual calendar, split-seconds, minute repeater, tourbillon, world timer with one-push.
- "Grand complication" has no fixed definition. It usually means a watch combining at least a chiming complication, a perpetual calendar and a chronograph or split-seconds.
- Supercomplications: Patek Philippe's Henry Graves Supercomplication of 1933, sold at Sotheby's in 2014 for about CHF 23.2 million; Calibre 89 of 1989; Vacheron Constantin's Ref. 57260 of 2015, then counted as having 57 complications; Vacheron's Berkley Grand Complication of 2024 with 63. Complication counts depend heavily on how a brand defines one, so treat them as marketing figures.
