# Authentication

Core rule: Claude does not certify authenticity from photos or descriptions. It can assess consistency and flag red flags, then direct the user to physical verification. State this plainly whenever an answer could influence a purchase.

## 1. The spectrum of "not right"

| Category | What it is | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Counterfeit, low grade | Obvious fakes with cheap movements | Usually detectable from photos: wrong fonts, cheap finishing, wrong movement. |
| Replica or "rep" | Better-made fakes, often with Chinese movements | Detectable by movement inspection and details. |
| Superclone | High-end fakes with cloned movements, correct weights and materials | Can fool photos and casual inspection; may need a movement check by an expert, or brand verification. |
| Franken watch | A watch assembled from genuine and non-genuine parts, or parts from different references | Common in vintage. May be all genuine parts that never belonged together. |
| Redial | A refinished or reprinted original dial | Ranges from honest restoration to deliberate fakery. |
| Relume | New luminous material on old hands or dial | Destroys originality, often obvious by color mismatch. |
| Service parts | Genuine replacement dials, hands, bezels or crowns fitted at service | Legitimate but lower value than original parts on collectible pieces. |
| Aftermarket modification | Non-factory diamonds, dials, bezels, coatings or bracelets on a genuine watch | Often voids warranty; brand service may refuse or replace parts. |
| Fake papers and boxes | Counterfeit warranty cards, certificates and boxes | Papers do not prove a watch is genuine. |
| Genuine but misdescribed | Real watch with the wrong year, wrong reference claim or undisclosed service parts | Very common; often seller error rather than fraud. |

## 2. Evidence hierarchy

From strongest to weakest:

1. Brand verification: service center inspection, or a brand archive extract matching the serials.
2. Expert physical inspection: an independent watchmaker or authenticator opening the watch and comparing every component with known references.
3. Physical checks by the buyer: weight, movement view, loupe inspection of printing and engravings.
4. Detailed high-resolution photos compared with verified references.
5. Seller reputation and documentation.
6. Price: a very low price is a warning, but a high price is not proof.

## 3. Red flags by area

### Overall
- Price far below the market for the claimed condition and set.
- Seller unwilling to provide specific photos, movement photos or a video.
- Story-heavy listings, urgency, unusual payment requests.

### Dial and printing
- Fonts slightly off in weight, spacing or serif shape; misaligned text; uneven printing edges under a loupe.
- Logo proportions wrong, or applied logos with poor finishing.
- Lume color that does not match the era, or hands and dial plots that do not match each other.
- Date font, date wheel alignment, and date magnification. Rolex's Cyclops magnifies about 2.5 times; weak magnification is a classic sign of a fake.
- Missing microprint or security details that the genuine dial has, such as Rolex's tiny crown at 6 o'clock etched in the crystal on modern models.

### Case and bezel
- Wrong case shape, chamfers, lug geometry or crown guards for the reference.
- Engravings that look stamped or shallow when they should be crisp, or vice versa.
- Bezel insert fonts, colors, ceramic quality, or click count and feel wrong. A 120-click bezel on a model that should have 60 clicks, or vice versa.
- Caseback type wrong for the model. Most Rolex models have solid casebacks; display backs appear only on specific recent models such as the Perpetual 1908 and some Daytonas. On other models a display back is a red flag.

### Movement
- The wrong caliber for the reference, or a caliber with the wrong bridge shapes, finishing, jewel count or engravings.
- Beat rate mismatch: listen or count. A 28,800 vph watch ticks eight times per second. Some fakes run at 21,600 vph, six ticks per second. Superclones may match.
- Decorated generic movements made to look like brand calibers.

### Bracelet, clasp and crown
- Light or rattly bracelets where the original should be solid; wrong clasp codes or engravings; poor machining on the clasp.
- Crown logos and dot or line markings wrong for the model, such as Rolex Twinlock and Triplock indicators.

### Papers and box
- Wrong card style for the year, mismatched serial numbers, misspellings, stamps from dealers that did not exist at that time.

## 4. Tools a buyer can use

- A 10x loupe for printing, engravings and finishing.
- An accurate scale; compare with known genuine weights, keeping in mind bracelet length.
- A timegrapher or timegrapher app to check beat rate.
- A UV light to compare lume reaction between hands and dial.
- A compass to check whether the watch is magnetized, not for authenticity.
- Reference photos from trusted dealers, auction catalogs and brand sites.

Avoid scratching, acid-testing or opening a watch the buyer does not own.

## 5. Professional verification

- Brand service centers: the most authoritative check. Be aware that brands may refuse to return counterfeit watches in some jurisdictions, and they may replace non-original parts during service.
- Independent watchmakers and authenticators with experience in that brand.
- Auction house specialists, especially for consignment-grade pieces.
- Platform programs: eBay's Authenticity Guarantee for watches over a threshold price in some markets, Chrono24 buyer protection and certified programs, and dealer guarantees. Check current terms.
- Archive extracts from the brand to confirm that serials and original configuration match.

## 6. Talking about results

Use calibrated language:

- "Everything visible is consistent with a genuine 1970s 1675": supportive, not a guarantee.
- "These three details are inconsistent with the reference": specific and actionable.
- "I can't assess this from the photos; the movement and the caseback interior would settle it": a request for evidence.

Always end with the recommended verification path when money is at stake.
